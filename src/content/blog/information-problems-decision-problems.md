A distinction I keep returning to: most products that feel disappointing are solving an information problem when the customer actually has a decision problem.

It's easiest to see through examples, so that's where I'll start.

**First example.**

Observation: a fleet operator has dashboards showing utilisation, fuel consumption, idle time, route performance and driver behaviour. They open them roughly never.

The obvious conclusion is that the dashboards need work. Better filters. Better charts. Maybe a mobile view.

The non-obvious conclusion is that the dashboard already answers the question it was built to answer — _what is happening?_ — and that was never the question the operator had. They wanted to know what to care about this week. No amount of charting gets you from the first question to the second.

**Second example.**

Observation: someone opens a recipe app, searches, scrolls through forty results, closes the app, and cooks the same thing they cooked on Tuesday.

The obvious conclusion is that the search results weren't good enough.

The non-obvious conclusion is that they were far too good. The app returned forty viable options to a person whose actual problem was that they had to pick one, given the vegetables that would spoil tomorrow, the thirty minutes available, and the fact that nobody wanted dal again. More options made the decision harder, not easier.

**Third example.**

Observation: a business sets up a folder, emails a customer asking for six documents, and then spends two weeks chasing the four that never arrived.

The obvious conclusion is that file sharing needs to be easier.

The non-obvious conclusion is that file sharing was already easy. What was hard was knowing whether the process was finished. The product modelled documents; the customer was tracking an outcome. Every one of those chase emails is the customer manually doing the accounting the software declined to do.

**Fourth example.**

Observation: someone watches product videos for thirty minutes and buys nothing.

The obvious conclusion is that engagement is healthy and monetisation needs tuning.

The non-obvious conclusion is that thirty minutes of watching may be evidence of a failure, not a success. The session produced no decision. If the feed had shown one genuinely relevant thing in the first four minutes, the session would have been shorter and worth more. Optimising watch time would systematically select against that outcome.

Why do most teams miss this?

Two reasons.

The first is that information problems are tractable and decision problems are not. You can scope a dashboard. You can estimate a search improvement. "Help the customer decide" doesn't decompose neatly into tickets, so it loses to work that does.

The second is that our metrics are mostly information-shaped. Queries served, reports viewed, sessions, dwell time, documents uploaded. Every one of those goes up when you give people more to process. None of them goes down when the customer still can't decide. You can move all of them and change nothing that matters.

> More information reduces uncertainty. It does not produce a decision. Those are different jobs, and only one of them is what the customer hired you for.

So the next time a customer asks for more visibility, more reporting, another dashboard, take the request seriously — and then ask what they intend to do once they can see it. Sometimes the honest answer is that they'll look at it, feel briefly informed, and carry on exactly as before.

That's the tell.

Because the goal was never to help people understand their situation better.

It was to help them do something about it.

[^1]: The obvious objection: sometimes people genuinely do want the raw data — analysts, auditors, anyone whose job is the investigation itself. That's real, and for those users an information product is the right product. The failure mode is assuming everyone is that user because they're the ones who file the feature requests.
