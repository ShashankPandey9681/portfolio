<p class="provenance">An update to a habit I used to recommend confidently, and now recommend carefully.</p>

For a long time I had two protected hours at the start of the day, and they were the most valuable thing on my calendar.

The shape of them was fixed. Roughly an hour of actual strategy work. Thirty minutes of strategic communication — the messages to stakeholders that set up the rest of the week. Then thirty minutes on the small set of items that genuinely only I could do.

Around that sat the rest of a morning routine: exercise first, a scroll through the news somewhere in there, and then the block.

It worked. I'd have written exactly the post you'd expect — protect your mornings, make them non-negotiable, everything else can wait.

Then my son was born, and the mornings stopped being mine.

## What the baby did to it

I want to be precise about this, because "I had less time" undersells it.

The problem isn't volume. It's that the time became **unpredictable**. Some mornings he's sound asleep and the house is silent at 5:40. Other mornings he's wide awake and radiating energy, as though he'd found an espresso somewhere in the night. There is no pattern I've been able to detect, and I've looked.

A non-negotiable 7 AM block cannot survive that. Not because you lack discipline — because the thing it depends on, a reliably empty hour arriving at a known time, is now decided by someone else.

So the block lost most of its efficacy before I worked out what to change. For a while I kept trying to run the old version, failing a few mornings a week, and treating that as a personal failure rather than a design problem.

## What actually survived

The fix, in the end, was to stop protecting the *slot* and start protecting the *block*.

It now runs in one of two windows: 5:30–7:30, or 9–11. Which one I get is determined in the first few minutes of being awake, by circumstances I don't control. I no longer plan which. I just know it'll be one of them, and whichever it turns out to be, that's where the discipline goes.

<p class="pullquote">The habit survived by giving up its time slot,<br />
not its length.</p>

The contents narrowed too, and this part I'd defend on its own merits.

The two hours are now almost entirely strategy work and strategy docs. The thirty minutes of stakeholder communication moved out and became async — written, sent whenever, not occupying prime cognitive hours. The "only I can do this" bucket mostly dissolved into things I now delegate, which is a change I wrote about elsewhere and which started well before the block was under threat.

What's left is the single category where uninterrupted time is non-substitutable. You can write a Slack update in fragments. You cannot think through a product strategy in eleven-minute increments, and no amount of willpower makes that false.

## What it displaced, honestly

Two things fell out of the morning.

The news scroll went, and I don't miss it. If anything the surprise has been how little I lost — almost nothing I actually needed arrived through that channel, and it was mostly a way of feeling informed while warming up.

Exercise also went, and that one I'm not going to file under "for the best".

I spent my early life as a competitive athlete, and I've got an ACL, a back and an ankle that all have opinions about neglect. Losing the morning session isn't a neutral trade I made deliberately — it's a cost I absorbed because something had to give, and it's the thing I'd most like back. I haven't solved it. I'm not going to write a tidy paragraph claiming I've found a clever substitute, because I haven't, and anyone in the same position would see through it immediately.

So: the block survived, and the body paid part of the bill. That's the actual trade, and I'd rather state it than dress it up as prioritisation.

## What I'd say now

I used to think the lesson here was discipline — hold the line, let the world wait, it always does.

I now think the lesson is narrower and less flattering.

A productivity habit that depends on your circumstances staying constant isn't a habit. It's a privilege you've mistaken for a system, and you find out which one it was the first time your life changes shape.

What makes this one durable isn't that I defend 7 AM. It's that I've identified the one activity that genuinely requires unbroken time, stripped everything else out of the window, and accepted that the window itself moves.

If you're trying to build something similar, I'd skip the fixed hour. Work out which single category of your work actually breaks when it's interrupted. Protect two hours for that, wherever those two hours land that day.

And be honest about what the two hours cost you. Something always pays for them. Mine is currently my knees.
